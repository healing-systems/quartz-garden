The practitioner is experimenting with digital minimalism (influenced by Cal Newport's work) after recognizing excessive phone use—social media, Spotify, video games—was stealing time from what they actually want to do: write, read, build businesses, rest intentionally. The insight came when they realized "there's so much to do and there's so much I want to do," but phone time was preventing both.

The intervention wasn't adding more productivity but creating a schedule to "remember what I actually care about." This reframes scheduling not as constraint but as memory—a tool to honor desires rather than maximize output. Key phrase: **"I'm not living to work. I'm working to live."**

The practitioner notes they're "running a couple of businesses" plus side projects, which "feel fulfilling but sometimes also feel like chores and they feel like work, because it's important that I do it so it feels like it must be done." Even meaningful work can become obligatory when it crowds out leisure, rest, and unchosen creative time.

Digital minimalism here functions as boundary-setting with technology to protect sacred time—not sacred as in spiritual practice necessarily, but sacred as in chosen, valued, aligned with actual desires rather than default consumption. The phone represents the path of least resistance that leads away from intentional living.
